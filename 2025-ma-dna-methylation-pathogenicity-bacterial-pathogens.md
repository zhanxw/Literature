## Main Idea
This minireview argues that bacterial DNA methylation is a major regulator of pathogenicity. It synthesizes evidence that methyltransferases and their target motifs influence antimicrobial resistance, virulence gene expression, biofilm formation, adhesion, motility, and stress adaptation, making selected methyltransferases and methylation sites potential therapeutic targets or diagnostic biomarkers.

## Evidence Supporting the Main Idea
- The review summarizes methylation detection methods, including SMRT sequencing, bisulfite-based sequencing, ultrafast bisulfite sequencing, enzymatic methylation sequencing, and nanopore sequencing.
- The paper emphasizes that the three major bacterial DNA methylation types are 6mA, 5mC, and 4mC, and notes method-specific strengths: SMRT is especially sensitive for 6mA, while nanopore sequencing is useful for detecting multiple modification types from native DNA.
- For antimicrobial resistance, the review gives examples across multiple pathogens: AveC4I in Aeromonas veronii affects chemotaxis-related regulators; ModA13 in Neisseria gonorrhoeae affects MtrF expression and Triton X-100 resistance; M.NgoAV knockout changes susceptibility to imipenem, cefotaxime, azithromycin, and bacitracin; ModS2 affects ampicillin resistance in Streptococcus suis; and ModP affects antibiotic resistance in Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae.
- Orphan methyltransferase examples include HsdM in Mycobacterium bovis, Dam and Dcm in E. coli, and Dam-linked persister and DNA repair responses in E. coli UTI89 and Acinetobacter baumannii.
- Figure 2 summarizes examples of methylation involvement in drug resistance, including the increased E. coli susceptibility to beta-lactams and quinolones after combined dam and recA knockout.
- For virulence gene expression, the review discusses SpyMEW123I in Streptococcus pyogenes, ModA13 and ModA16 in Haemophilus influenzae group aegyptius, Dam in Salmonella typhimurium, and other methyltransferases that regulate virulence factors or host interaction.
- Figure 3 summarizes methylation effects on virulence gene expression, including Dam effects on Salmonella colonization and SPI-1 secretion.
- For biofilm, adhesion, motility, and adaptation, the review discusses Dam regulation of O-antigen chain length in Salmonella enterica, agn43 expression in E. coli, adhesion and invasion phenotypes in H. influenzae, fimbrial operon control in S. typhimurium, and methylation-linked motility or environmental-stress responses.
- Figure 5 integrates the review's model: DNA methylation influences resistance, adaptability to stressors such as hypoxia and oxidative stress, virulence gene expression, biofilm formation, adhesion, and motility.

## Main Novelty
The paper's contribution is a focused synthesis of DNA methylation as a regulator of bacterial pathogenicity. It organizes scattered evidence into a pathogenicity-centered framework and argues that key methyltransferases and methylation motifs could become antimicrobial targets or infection biomarkers.

## Key Biology Insights
- DNA methylation is not only a restriction-modification defense system; it also regulates pathogenic traits.
- The methyltransferases relevant to pathogenicity vary by species and even by strain, so there is no established universal pathogenicity methyltransferase.
- Both R-M-associated methyltransferases and orphan methyltransferases such as Dam, Dcm, and CcrM can influence bacterial phenotypes.
- Methylation may regulate pathogenicity through promoter accessibility, transcription factor binding, phase-variable regulons, DNA repair pathways, persister formation, and modulation of surface structures.
- The field still lacks enough mechanistic studies linking specific methylation sites to direct regulatory outcomes in many pathogens.

## Implications
- Methyltransferases and methylation motifs may become useful antimicrobial targets, especially when perturbing them weakens virulence or restores drug susceptibility.
- Pathogenicity-associated methylation sites could become biomarkers for diagnosing, monitoring, or stratifying bacterial infections.
- Future work needs paired methylome, transcriptome, phenotype, and infection-model experiments to distinguish direct methylation effects from indirect regulatory consequences.
