## Main Idea
- The authors propose that aminoglycosides are a class of small-molecule RNA condensers, extending their established role as multivalent RNA-binding antibiotics to macroscopic RNA phase separation.
- Neomycin B (neoB) is used as the representative compound. According to the v2 abstract, neoB induces concentration-dependent phase separation of poly(A), poly(U), and total *E. coli* RNA.

## Evidence Supporting the Main Idea
- NeoB induces RNA condensation in multiple RNA substrates: poly(A), poly(U), and total *E. coli* RNA.
- The abstract reports that kanamycin, apramycin, and gentamicin also condense RNA, supporting a class-level rather than compound-specific effect.
- Condensate size and density vary with pH and ionic strength, consistent with contributions from neoB protonation and electrostatic screening.
- NeoB condensates are reported to be more stable than spermine condensates even though spermine has at least as much effective charge at physiological pH.
- Fucitol, a hydroxyl-rich polyol without amines, does not condense RNA, supporting a requirement for cationic amine groups.
- Molecular-dynamics simulations attribute neoB's greater condensation efficiency to additional hydrogen bonds donated by its hydroxyl groups.
- The detailed figures, tables, sample sizes, concentrations, and statistical tests were not available because the bioRxiv full-text endpoint was rate-limited during retrieval; the points above are restricted to the v2 abstract and public metadata.

## Main Novelty
- Demonstrates that aminoglycoside antibiotics can drive macroscopic RNA phase separation.
- Separates the roles of cationic amines and hydroxyl groups using spermine and fucitol comparisons.
- Links the molecular features that support tight aminoglycoside-RNA recognition to condensate formation and stability.

## Datasets Used for Evaluation
- Poly(A) RNA: synthetic homopolymeric RNA substrate used to test neoB-induced condensation; sample size and preparation details were not specified in the accessible abstract.
- Poly(U) RNA: synthetic homopolymeric RNA substrate used to test neoB-induced condensation; sample size and preparation details were not specified in the accessible abstract.
- Total *E. coli* RNA: cellular RNA mixture used as a more complex condensation substrate; sample size and preparation details were not specified in the accessible abstract.
- Aminoglycosides (neoB, kanamycin, apramycin, and gentamicin), spermine, and fucitol: chemical comparators used to evaluate compound class effects and the contributions of charge, amines, and hydroxyl groups.
- Molecular-dynamics simulation systems: used to compare molecular interactions underlying condensation efficiency; system sizes and simulation trajectories were not specified in the accessible abstract.

## Experimental Procedure
- Test whether neoB produces concentration-dependent phase separation with poly(A), poly(U), and total *E. coli* RNA.
- Vary pH and ionic strength to assess effects on condensate size and density.
- Compare neoB with spermine as a polycationic RNA-condensing molecule.
- Test kanamycin, apramycin, and gentamicin to determine whether RNA condensation extends across aminoglycosides.
- Use fucitol as an amine-free, hydroxyl-rich control.
- Apply molecular-dynamics simulations to examine hydrogen-bonding and other interactions that may explain neoB's condensation efficiency.
- Exact protocols, imaging/quantification methods, replicate numbers, and statistical analyses were not specified in the accessible abstract.

## Key Biology Insights
- Aminoglycosides may alter RNA organization not only through sequence- or structure-selective binding but also by driving RNA into condensed phases.
- Positive amine groups appear necessary for condensation, while hydroxyl groups can strengthen the interaction through hydrogen bonding.
- RNA condensation may contribute to aminoglycoside bactericidal activity and cellular toxicity, although this proposed physiological connection is not directly established by the abstract-level evidence.

## Implications
- RNA condensation provides a potential physicochemical mechanism linking aminoglycoside chemical structure to antibiotic activity and toxicity.
- The findings may inform the design of RNA-binding small molecules with tunable condensation properties.
- Cellular relevance, effects on specific RNA classes, and whether condensation occurs at pharmacologically relevant intracellular concentrations require further full-text and in vivo evaluation.
