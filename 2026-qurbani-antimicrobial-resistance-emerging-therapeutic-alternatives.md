# Paper Summary

### Authors
Karzan Qurbani, Rahel Khdhr, Karokh Khdir, Safin Hussein, Hiwa Mohammed Qadr, Abdulmalik Fareeq Saber, and Sangar Ali.

### Journal
*Biomedical Materials Science*, 1:8 (2026).

### Publication Date
2026-09-04.

### DOI
[10.1186/s44495-026-00009-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s44495-026-00009-y)

## Keywords

Antimicrobial resistance; non-antibiotic therapies; nanoparticles; CRISPR-Cas antimicrobials; antimicrobial peptides; bacteriophages; biomaterials; drug delivery; translational limitations.

## Main Idea

This critical narrative review evaluates emerging non-antibiotic approaches for antimicrobial resistance (AMR), including nanoparticles, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), bacteriophages, probiotics and postbiotics, CRISPR-Cas systems, immune modulators, plant-derived antimicrobials, and biomaterial-based delivery platforms. The central conclusion is that these approaches are unlikely to replace conventional antibiotics universally. Their most credible near-term value is as targeted, localized, or adjunctive interventions that diversify antimicrobial mechanisms, disrupt biofilms, reduce antibiotic exposure, and address selected resistant infections.

The review organizes each strategy around three questions: its mechanism and activity spectrum, the strength of in vivo or clinical evidence, and barriers to translation such as toxicity, delivery, manufacturing, regulation, and resistance evolution.

## Evidence Supporting the Main Idea

- Figure 1 and Table 2 link major AMR mechanisms—reduced uptake, active efflux, enzymatic inactivation, target modification, biofilm tolerance, and acquired resistance determinants—to potential countermeasures. Nanoparticles and membrane-active AMPs can act at the bacterial envelope; phages can provide pathogen-specific killing; and CRISPR systems can target resistance-associated genes or mobile elements.
- The review reports that nanoparticles can damage membranes, release metal ions, inhibit enzymes and DNA/protein synthesis, interfere with efflux, and penetrate or disrupt biofilms. It also notes dose-dependent host toxicity, bioaccumulation, instability, manufacturing, and ecological concerns.
- Table 3 summarizes preclinical examples with in vivo evidence, including a Pseudomonas phage that increased survival in mice, ciprofloxacin-PEG-ZnO nanoparticles that showed antibacterial and wound-healing activity in mice, and several localized nanoparticle or gel formulations. Many other entries are explicitly limited to in vitro or non-animal models.
- Table 5 shows uneven clinical maturity. Examples include the PhagoBurn phase I/II study, a phase III trial of topical peceleganan (PL-5) in 570 patients with secondary skin-wound infections, a randomized trial of a silver nanoparticle wound formulation in 86 patients, the phase II LBP-EC01 CRISPR-Cas3-enhanced phage study, and phase II development of BX004 inhaled phage therapy. The review emphasizes that clinical feasibility or improvement does not necessarily establish microbiological eradication or replacement of antibiotics.
- The review's critical appraisal notes that many studies use highly controlled in vitro assays or acute murine models that may not reproduce polymicrobial infection, host immunity, tissue penetration, pharmacokinetic variability, toxicity, or relapse. It also highlights inconsistent reporting of MIC, MBC, and biofilm assays.
- Figure 2 presents the proposed landscape of complementary strategies: nanoparticles, AMPs, phages, CRISPR, immune modulation, probiotics/postbiotics, plant compounds, and photodynamic or sonodynamic adjuncts.

## Main Novelty

The review's main contribution is a translational comparison rather than a new antimicrobial agent or experiment. It applies one framework across mechanistically diverse strategies and explicitly separates laboratory promise, animal evidence, and human clinical evaluation. It also integrates biomaterials—coatings, hydrogels, scaffolds, and controlled-release systems—as a bridge between antimicrobial discovery and localized clinical use.

## Datasets Used for Evaluation

No experimental datasets were generated or analyzed in this review. The evidence base was assembled through searches of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar using Boolean combinations of AMR and non-antibiotic therapy terms. The main search covered publications from January 2015 through June 2025; landmark earlier studies were selectively retained when foundational. Two authors performed title/abstract screening independently, with disagreements resolved by a third author. The paper does not report a dataset-level record count or formal quantitative meta-analysis.

## Experimental Procedure

- Define AMR mechanisms and clinical/economic context, including reduced uptake, efflux, enzymatic inactivation, target modification, biofilms, and horizontal gene transfer.
- Search four electronic databases with AMR and intervention-specific Boolean terms; use supplementary searches for categories such as bacteriophage clinical outcomes, CRISPR delivery, and AMP translation.
- Include peer-reviewed original studies or systematic reviews addressing mechanisms, efficacy, or translational limitations of non-traditional antimicrobials; exclude conference abstracts, editorials, letters, and non-English articles.
- Screen titles and abstracts, then assess eligible full texts. When multiple studies covered the same strategy, prioritize stronger in vivo evidence or greater translational relevance.
- Synthesize evidence qualitatively by therapeutic category and classify examples as discovery-stage, preclinical/in vivo, or human clinical development.
- Compare mechanisms, targeted pathogens, safety and delivery considerations, manufacturing/regulatory barriers, and the possibility of resistance development. Tables 1–6 provide mechanistic, preclinical, safety, clinical, and global-policy comparisons.

## Key Biology Insights

- Biofilms combine an extracellular polymeric-substance barrier, slow-growing or dormant persister cells, altered metabolism, and enhanced horizontal gene transfer; this makes them a major target for nanoparticles, AMPs, phages, chitosan systems, coatings, and hydrogels.
- Multi-target membrane disruption and reactive oxygen species may reduce dependence on conventional antibiotic targets, but they do not make resistance impossible. The review identifies membrane remodeling, efflux changes, stress responses, receptor mutation, CRISPR counter-defense, and ecological adaptation as plausible escape routes.
- Phages and CRISPR-based systems offer specificity, but that specificity creates delivery, receptor, polymicrobial-infection, and resistance-countermeasure challenges.
- Localized biomaterials can combine antimicrobial activity with controlled release, retention at infected tissue, reduced systemic exposure, and—in some implant or scaffold designs—tissue integration or regeneration.
- The strongest clinical opportunities currently appear to be indication-specific and localized, such as wound care, device-associated infection, and inhaled or urinary tract applications, rather than broad systemic replacement therapy.

## Implications

Non-antibiotic strategies should be developed as antibiotic-sparing or combination tools within antimicrobial stewardship, not marketed as universally “resistance-proof” substitutes. Clinical programs should prioritize well-defined patient populations, optimized dose and delivery, clinically meaningful microbiological and patient-centered endpoints, pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic characterization, reproducible manufacturing, and long-term safety and ecological assessment. Regulatory pathways must accommodate biologics, phages, nanomaterials, living therapeutics, and combination products. The review further recommends embedding innovation within a One Health framework linking human, animal, and environmental surveillance, responsible antimicrobial use, wastewater control, and agricultural policy.
