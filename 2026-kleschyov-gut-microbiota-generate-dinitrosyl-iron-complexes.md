## Keywords
Gut microbiota; dinitrosyl iron complexes (DNICs); nitrate; non-heme iron; nitric oxide signaling; soluble guanylyl cyclase; mTORC1; steatosis; cardiometabolic health.

## Main Idea
The authors report a gut-microbe-to-host pathway in which bacteria convert inorganic nitrate and non-heme iron into mobile bioactive dinitrosyl iron complexes. These DNICs enter systemic tissues and improve cardiometabolic phenotypes through signaling effects that depend on the Fe(NO)2 entity, rather than on free nitric oxide alone.

## Evidence Supporting the Main Idea
- Electron paramagnetic resonance detected DNICs in tissues from conventional mice but not germ-free mice, linking DNIC formation to the microbiota.
- Mouse and human fecal material and *E. coli* generated DNICs when supplied with nitrate and iron citrate; a nitrate-reductase-deficient *E. coli* mutant did not.
- In Western-diet-fed mice, dietary nitrate plus iron citrate or synthetic DNICs increased tissue DNIC levels and ameliorated cardiometabolic dysfunction.
- DNICs reduced fatty-acid-induced steatosis in HepG2 cells and human hepatocyte spheroids.
- Mechanistically, DNIC activity involved soluble guanylyl cyclase activation, reduced leucine uptake, and normalization of mTORC1 signaling.
- Evidence and numerical details in this summary are taken from the published abstract; the publisher's full-text endpoint was not retrievable in this workflow, so figure- and table-specific values are not reported.

## Main Novelty
The study identifies DNICs as a mobile chemical output of gut microbial nitrate/iron metabolism and connects this metabolite pathway to systemic cardiometabolic regulation. It also distinguishes DNIC Fe(NO)2 signaling from effects attributable to free NO.

## Datasets Used for Evaluation
- **Conventional and germ-free mice:** tissue samples used to test whether DNICs are microbiota-dependent. Sample size: Not specified in paper abstract.
- **Western-diet-fed mice:** an in vivo disease-model cohort used to evaluate whether nitrate plus iron citrate or synthetic DNICs improve cardiometabolic dysfunction. Sample size: Not specified in paper abstract.
- **Mouse and human feces:** ex vivo biological material used to test microbial DNIC production from nitrate and iron citrate. Sample size: Not specified in paper abstract.
- ***E. coli* and a nitrate-reductase-deficient mutant:** bacterial cultures used to test the requirement for nitrate reduction. Replicate/sample size: Not specified in paper abstract.
- **HepG2 cells and human hepatocyte spheroids:** in vitro and 3D human-cell models used to evaluate protection from fatty-acid-induced steatosis and downstream signaling. Replicate/sample size: Not specified in paper abstract.

## Experimental Procedure
- Detect DNICs in tissues from conventional versus germ-free mice using electron paramagnetic resonance.
- Incubate mouse feces, human feces, and *E. coli* with nitrate and iron citrate; compare DNIC generation with a nitrate-reductase-deficient mutant.
- Supplement Western-diet-fed mice with nitrate plus iron citrate or administer synthetic DNICs, then measure tissue DNICs and cardiometabolic outcomes.
- Expose HepG2 cells and human hepatocyte spheroids to fatty acids with or without DNICs and assess steatosis.
- Dissect mechanism by testing the Fe(NO)2 moiety, soluble guanylyl cyclase activation, leucine uptake, and mTORC1 signaling.

## Key Biology Insights
- Gut bacteria can transform dietary/inorganic nitrogen and iron chemistry into a systemically distributed signaling species.
- Microbiota-dependent nitrate reduction is necessary for the observed DNIC-generating activity in the tested bacterial system.
- DNICs may couple gut microbial metabolism to liver lipid handling and broader cardiometabolic physiology.
- The signaling entity is chemically distinct from free NO, suggesting that microbiome-derived NO-related metabolites may have separable biological functions.

## Implications
Modulating gut-microbial DNIC formation, or delivering DNIC-like compounds, could become a strategy for supporting cardiometabolic health. The findings are preclinical: the abstract reports mouse, bacterial, and cell/spheroid experiments, not a human intervention trial. Future work should define the responsible microbial taxa and enzymes, dose-response and safety, tissue pharmacokinetics, and whether the pathway is therapeutically reproducible in humans.
