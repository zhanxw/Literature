## Main Idea
This review explains how DNA methylation forms the core of the bacterial epigenome. It argues that bacterial methylation controls DNA-protein interactions, genome defense, replication, repair, transcription, and phase-variable formation of phenotypic subpopulations that help bacteria adapt to changing environments and host niches.

## Evidence Supporting the Main Idea
- The review describes the three main bacterial methylated bases: 6mA, 5mC, and 4mC. It notes that 4mC is found in bacteria and archaea, while 6mA and 5mC also occur in eukaryotes.
- The article highlights SMRT sequencing and nanopore sequencing as major technologies that made genome-wide bacterial methylome analysis practical.
- It explains that bacterial DNA methyltransferases can act as both de novo writers and maintenance copyists because they can methylate unmethylated and hemi-methylated DNA.
- The review distinguishes restriction-modification-associated methyltransferases from orphan methyltransferases, while emphasizing that this distinction is not functionally absolute: some R-M methyltransferases regulate transcription and phenotypic variation, and orphan methyltransferases are abundant.
- Examples of writer-reader systems include Dam, Dcm, and CcrM methyltransferases and readers such as DnaA, SeqA, MutHLS, RNA polymerase, transposases, and transcription factors.
- The review describes how transient hemi-methylation after DNA replication can become stable when DNA-binding proteins block remethylation of selected sites.
- Dam-dependent phase variation examples include E. coli pap and agn43, Salmonella gtr, opvAB, and std loci. These systems generate heritable but reversible subpopulations.
- The Salmonella opvAB example illustrates a tradeoff between virulence and phage resistance: one state shortens O-antigen and reduces virulence but improves phage resistance, while switching back restores virulence after phage pressure changes.
- The Salmonella std example illustrates division of labor during infection: one state is motile and invasive, while another state is fimbrial and better suited to adhesion in the large intestine.
- Phase-variable methyltransferases in type I, type II, and type III R-M systems are discussed as phasevarion generators in pathogens such as Haemophilus influenzae, H. pylori, Neisseria species, Campylobacter jejuni, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Mycoplasma pulmonis.
- The review links DNA methylation to virulence attenuation and therapeutic possibilities, including dam-mutant live vaccines, DNA methyltransferase inhibitors, antibiotic potentiation, phage therapy, and methylation-based biosensors.

## Main Novelty
The review integrates bacterial DNA methylation into a broad epigenome framework. Its main contribution is showing that DNA methylation is not a narrow restriction-defense feature, but a general information layer that can generate heritable expression states, adaptive subpopulations, and pathogen-host interaction differences.

## Datasets Used for Evaluation
- Not a new experimental dataset. This is a review of historical and contemporary bacterial methylation literature.
- The evidence base spans model bacteria and pathogens including E. coli, Salmonella enterica, Caulobacter crescentus, Yersinia species, Haemophilus influenzae, H. pylori, Neisseria meningitidis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Campylobacter jejuni, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Mycoplasma pulmonis, and others.

## Experimental Procedure
- Not applicable as a primary experimental protocol; the article is a conceptual review.
- Summarize bacterial methylated bases and DNA methyltransferase classes.
- Compare bacterial and eukaryotic epigenome logic.
- Review mechanisms of hemi-methylation, passive demethylation, and methylation-dependent transcriptional regulation.
- Synthesize examples of Dam-dependent bistability and phase variation.
- Review phase-variable R-M methyltransferases and phasevarions.
- Discuss evolutionary models and possible biomedical applications.

## Key Biology Insights
- DNA methylation can create stable, heritable regulatory states without changing nucleotide sequence.
- In bacteria, methylation memory is usually erased passively by DNA replication when remethylation is blocked, rather than by dedicated demethylases.
- Methylation-dependent phase variation can create phenotypic diversity that acts as bet hedging or division of labor.
- Phasevarions can coordinate switching of many genes at once, making methyltransferase phase variation a high-impact regulatory mechanism.
- Horizontal transfer and recombination of methyltransferase target-recognition domains can diversify methylomes and potentially create new bacterial lineages or regulatory states.
- Methylation can be both adaptive and risky: for example, 5mC can deaminate to thymine, creating mutational pressure if not repaired.

## Implications
- Bacterial methylomes should be interpreted as dynamic regulatory systems, not just catalogs of modified bases.
- Understanding methylation readers, target motifs, and phase-variable methyltransferases can clarify how pathogens adapt to host environments, immune pressure, phages, and antibiotics.
- Therapeutic strategies targeting bacterial methylation are plausible, but host effects and species-specific methyltransferase functions need careful evaluation.
- Future functional methylome studies are likely to reveal additional roles in cell cycle control, DNA repair, virulence secretion, and pathogen-host epigenetic interactions.
