## Main Idea

This Review argues that human antiviral immunity and bacterial anti-phage defence share ancient, conserved design principles. Across kingdoms of life, antiviral systems can be understood through three recurring functions: pathogen recognition, signal amplification and immune effector action. The review highlights cGAS–STING, inflammasomes, Argonautes and viperin as examples of related defence mechanisms found in both bacteria and eukaryotes.

## Evidence Supporting the Main Idea

- The abstract reports that components of human antiviral immunity originated billions of years ago in bacterial pathways that defend against phage replication.
- Cross-kingdom comparisons connect human pattern-recognition receptors and interferon-stimulated genes with bacterial CRISPR immunity and other anti-phage systems.
- The review identifies shared strategies for sensing viral replication and converting detection into antiviral effector activity.
- The available abstract does not provide quantitative effect sizes or a single experimental dataset; this is a conceptual review synthesizing prior studies.

## Main Novelty

The review unifies previously separate literatures on human innate antiviral immunity and bacterial anti-phage defence into a common framework. Its central contribution is the proposal that pathogen sensing, signal transduction and effector function follow universal rules that can be studied across the tree of life.

## Experimental Procedure

- Compare antiviral and anti-phage mechanisms across bacteria, humans and other animals.
- Organize mechanisms by pathogen recognition, signal amplification and immune effector function.
- Examine conserved examples including cGAS–STING, inflammasomes, Argonautes and viperin.
- Relate mammalian pattern-recognition receptors and interferon-stimulated genes to bacterial CRISPR and anti-phage defence systems.
- Use the cross-kingdom comparison to identify general host–virus interaction rules and open questions.

## Key Biology Insights

- Antiviral defence is a conserved feature of cellular life, spanning bacteria and complex multicellular organisms.
- Recognition, amplification and effector action are recurring architectural layers of antiviral immunity.
- Some systems traditionally viewed as animal immune innovations have deep evolutionary roots in bacterial anti-phage defence.
- Comparing bacteria and eukaryotes may reveal both universal defence rules and lineage-specific adaptations.

## Implications

The framework may help researchers discover new immune systems by searching for bacterial counterparts of animal antiviral pathways and vice versa. It also provides an evolutionary basis for studying host–virus interactions, but the abstract alone does not establish that every mechanistic analogy is functionally interchangeable across organisms. The review explicitly leaves open questions about how broadly these shared principles apply and how they evolved.
